## Abstract
Formula 1 produces more data per race than almost any other sport, yet individual results still feel chaotic. This project uses eight seasons of official FIA timing data (2018–2025) - 149 race weekends, 2,977 driver-race entries - to answer four questions: whether the driver or the car drives race outcomes, whether the FIA's 2021 budget cap changed competitive balance, whether that cap narrowed the pace gap between teams, and how accurately a model can predict who wins on Sunday. Our ensemble ML model with walk-forward in-season updating reaches 59.2% winner accuracy - roughly 92% of the theoretical maximum, given that about 35% of races are affected by unpredictable mid-race events. Across all four analyses, car performance consistently accounts for more of race outcomes than driver skill, though driver ability shows up as a real, measurable signal.
---
## Introduction
Formula 1 is a sport where car design determines who can win before the first lap is even run, yet driver skill still shapes how close to that ceiling each team actually gets. Four questions drove this analysis:
1. **Driver vs. Car:** What fraction of race performance comes from the driver versus the machine?
2. **Cost Cap Impact:** Did the 2021 budget cap shift which teams were winning?
3. **Lap-Time Convergence:** Did the cap narrow the pace gap between fastest and slowest teams?
4. **Predictability:** How accurately can a model predict race winners, and how close to the theoretical ceiling?
---
## Data
We used the FastF1 Python library [1] for all data - official FIA timing, telemetry, and session results for 2018–2025, covering 2,977 driver-race entries across 149 rounds. One structural issue shapes every result in this report: the 2022 aerodynamic regulation overhaul happened simultaneously with the cost cap's full implementation, making it basically impossible to cleanly separate budget-driven performance changes from regulation-driven ones. We flag this wherever it matters. We also had minor data gaps - six corrupted sessions from 2018–2019, missing qualifying times from wet-weather sessions, and the abbreviated 2020 calendar - addressed by rolling-average imputation with binary flags marking each imputed row.

## Results
### RQ1 - Driver Skill vs. Car Performance
The tricky part about measuring driver skill is that every result is contaminated by the car. The cleanest separation we could find is teammate comparisons: two drivers in the same car during qualifying, where any consistent gap has to come from the driver rather than the machinery.
**Figure 1** shows qualifying time gaps between teammates across six constructors.
*Figure 1: Each dot is one qualifying session; horizontal position is the time gap in seconds between teammates (positive = Driver A faster, negative = Driver B faster). Red Bull's cluster consistently sits near +0.3s with tight spread - Verstappen almost always faster than Pérez by roughly the same margin; McLaren centers near zero with wider scatter; Williams and Haas show the widest distributions.*
**Figure 2** shows the distribution of throttle and brake application from raw telemetry, comparing race winners to the rest of the field.
*Figure 2: Vertical axis is throttle percentage; horizontal axis is brake pressure; brighter regions mean more time spent at that behavior. Winners concentrate in the upper-left quadrant (simultaneous throttle and brake - trail-braking); the field clusters in a non-overlapping brake-then-throttle pattern. This is the physical mechanism behind the qualifying gaps.*

### RQ2 - Cost Cap and Race Outcomes
Before 2021, the top three teams spent roughly $300–400M per year while backmarker teams spent $70–120M [13]. The cap set a hard limit of $145M for 2021, dropping to $135M by 2023.
**Figure 3** maps points concentration across all finishing positions and seasons.
*Figure 3: Darker color means more points scored at that position that year. Dark color clusters at P1–P3 before the 2022 dashed line; from 2022 onward, dark color spreads visibly into P4–P7 - more teams collecting meaningful points across more races.*
**Figure 4** compares on-track time gaps to the race winner, pre- and post-cap, by finishing position.
*Figure 4: Left halves (red) = 2018–2020; right halves (teal) = 2022–2025; horizontal axis is gap in seconds to the race winner. Median gaps at P8–P10 fell from 80–100s pre-cap to 40–60s post-cap; front-position gaps changed far less - the back of the field is where the biggest spending differences existed.*

### ML Model
It turns out yes - we can predict F1 race outcomes from pre-race data, and we got closer to the theoretical ceiling than we expected. The model combines four algorithms - XGBoost Regressor, XGBoost Ranker, Random Forest Classifier, and ExtraTrees Regressor - blended via Bayesian Model Averaging. A Walk-Forward Plackett-Luce layer sits on top and updates driver strength estimates after each race completes. We tested with Leave-One-Season-Out (LOSO) validation: train on six seasons, predict the seventh unseen, then rotate until every season has been held out (149 total holdout predictions). Random train/test splits would cheat here - F1 data has strong time patterns, and a model trained and tested on the same season would be quietly leaking future information. Full architecture and hyperparameters are in Appendix A; the Plackett-Luce derivation is in Appendix B.
**Figure 16** compares walk-forward and base model accuracy, race by race.
*Figure 16: Blue = walk-forward model, orange = base model, gray = oracle. The lines overlap for races 1–4; from race 5 onward blue consistently sits above orange and tracks closer to the gray oracle - in-season learning paying off.*
**Figure 17** compares all three models' accuracy by season.
*Figure 17: Season accuracy ranges from 45.5% (2022) to 86.4% (2023). Walk-forward averages 59.2%; base model averages 55.7%.*

The theoretical ceiling - estimated from observed safety car rates (~35% of races) [2], mechanical retirements (~7%) [3], and first-lap incidents (~15–20%) [4] - works out to approximately:
Ceiling ≈ 0.65 × 0.80 + 0.35 × 0.35 ≈ 0.64
Against that ceiling, 59.2% captures:
(59.2% - 5%) / (64% - 5%) ≈ 91.9%
of all predictable information above random guessing.

## Discussion
The teammate qualifying data shows gaps of 0.2–0.4 seconds per lap in identical hardware that repeat consistently across sessions - not just once. Red Bull's +0.3s delta between the same two drivers appeared session after session throughout an entire season, which is more consistent than equipment variation or setup luck would produce. The telemetry shows race winners applying throttle while still on the brakes, while the rest of the field uses a sequential brake-first pattern. These two observations are probably related - the trail-braking technique keeps weight loaded over the front tyres through the corner, which likely explains some of the consistent qualifying gap rather than it coming from faster reactions alone.
On the cost cap and convergence: the results are real but we want to be upfront about their limits. Both the points heatmap and the violin plot agree that the back of the field - where pre-cap spending differences were largest - showed the most time compression after 2021. That's the part of the pattern that's hard to attribute purely to the 2022 regulation change, since new technical rules don't specifically help the teams with the smallest budgets. Honestly, both forces probably contributed, and the 51% reduction in performance spread is a genuine outcome even if we can't untangle the exact cause. The data shows correlation, not causality.
The ML model's biggest accuracy gains from in-season updating show up in 2019 (+9.5%) and 2024 (+8.3%) - years when the season's form shifted mid-year relative to what historical data predicted. In stable years like 2023, the walk-forward layer added nothing because the hierarchy was already obvious from pre-race data. The 70/30 car-to-driver ratio the model learned independently also has real-world implications - driver market valuations built primarily on recent race results, without accounting for car performance, could be systematically misattributing credit.
---
## Conclusion
Eight seasons of F1 data gave us four fairly clear answers. Driver skill is real and shows up consistently - qualifying gaps of 0.2–0.4s in identical cars, backed up by concrete telemetry differences - but car performance accounts for roughly 70% of what predicts race results. The 2021 cost cap coincided with a 51% reduction in the performance spread between top and bottom constructors, though the 2022 regulation overhaul happening at the same time prevents any clean causal claim, and we want to be honest about that. Race outcomes are substantially predictable: 59.2% winner accuracy is approximately 92% of the theoretical ceiling imposed by race-day randomness - safety cars, mechanical failures, and first-lap incidents that no pre-race model could ever see coming. The car sets the ceiling, the driver determines how close to that ceiling the team operates, and about one in three races gets decided by something outside any model's reach.
---
## Further Research
- **Real-time strategy modeling:** Incorporating live safety car probability and pit window data could push accuracy above the ~64% pre-race ceiling
- **Driver aging curves:** A spline on driver age would capture physical performance decline documented in athletes past their mid-30s more precisely than career starts alone
- **Post-2026 recalibration:** The 2026 regulation overhaul will reset performance hierarchies as dramatically as 2022; new LOSO folds will be needed after 2026–2028 data accumulates
- **Causal cost cap analysis:** A regression discontinuity design around the 2021–2022 enforcement boundary, combined with FIA financial disclosures now publicly available, could better isolate the cap's effect from the regulation change
- **Cross-series transfer:** Testing whether skill signals generalize from F2 and Formula E data would determine whether these findings are specific to F1 or reflect broader motorsport patterns

## Challenge Goals
### Challenge Goal 1 - Machine Learning Model
**Goal and assessment.** The goal was to predict F1 race winners from pre-race data, measured by winner accuracy: the fraction of races where the model's predicted P1 matched the actual P1. Two explicit baselines: random guessing gives 5% (1 winner from 20 drivers), and always picking the championship leader gives roughly 40–45%. The theoretical upper bound (~64%) was estimated from observed rates of result-altering mid-race events - safety cars affecting ~35% of races [2], mechanical retirements at ~7% [3], and first-lap incidents at ~15–20% [4]. These are genuinely unpredictable before the race starts and represent irreducible noise, not model failures. Against that ceiling, the final walk-forward model captures 91.9% of all available predictable information above random guessing.
**Model architecture and comparison.** Four model types were explicitly trained and compared: XGBoost Regressor (minimizing positional error), XGBoost Ranker (NDCG ranking loss, optimized for top-3 ordering), Random Forest Classifier (estimating win probability per driver), and ExtraTrees Regressor (adding prediction diversity). Bayesian Model Averaging then derived weights from each model's cross-validated error variance - models with more consistent errors earn higher weight [9]. The final weights were nearly uniform (0.215–0.268), which actually reveals something: each model captures genuinely different aspects of the prediction problem rather than one dominating. Had a single model been clearly superior, BMA would have concentrated weight there. The Walk-Forward Plackett-Luce layer adds in-season adaptation, contributing 4 percentage points overall (55.1% base → 59.2% walk-forward), with the largest gains in transition years like 2019 (+9.5%) and 2024 (+8.3%).
**How the model makes its predictions.** Figure 24 (feature importances) is the clearest window into the model's decision-making. Car-pace inputs - qualifying gap to pole, FP2 long-run pace, team rolling form - account for approximately 70% of total prediction signal. Driver-specific inputs contribute the remaining 30%. Importantly, this ratio emerged from the data through LOSO cross-validation; the model was not told to prefer car inputs. It independently discovered the same hierarchy that the teammate comparison analysis found in RQ1, which makes both findings more credible. Figure 25 shows the model's internal confidence structure: tight diagonal bands for the top 4–5 drivers, appropriately wider spread for mid-pack drivers. Figure 26 (confusion matrix) shows the same story from a standings perspective - ranking errors cluster within 1–2 positions of the diagonal rather than appearing as random catastrophic mispredictions. Figure 18 shows the walk-forward layer tracking one driver's strength rising cleanly away from the field within 3 races in 2023, and Figure 19 shows the blend weight rising steeply from near-zero to its 0.45 cap. Together these explain the accuracy gains in Figure 16.
**Predictions about future situations.** Based on end-of-2024 race results, the walk-forward model assigned McLaren the highest rolling form score entering 2025, ahead of both Red Bull and Ferrari. For the 2026 season, the model would be expected to behave similarly to 2022: accuracy near the 45% floor in the first 4–6 races, recovering through the second half as the walk-forward layer learns the new car hierarchy. The 2022 season (45.5%) and 2023 season (86.4%) are empirical lower and upper bounds on the model's expected operating range.
**Biases and feedback loop potential.** The 70/30 car-to-driver split is the model's primary structural bias: drivers who outperform their equipment - consistently gaining positions in races versus their qualifying slot - are systematically underranked in pre-race predictions because the car-pace features dominate. A second bias affects rookies: their rolling-form windows are short or empty, pushing predictions toward the imputed field median regardless of actual talent. The James-Stein shrinkage addresses the small-sample circuit-history problem, but rolling form has no equivalent regularization. The main feedback loop risk: FP2 long-run pace accounts for ~20% of the prediction signal. If teams knew this, they could game Friday long-run pace to appear faster or slower than they actually are - this kind of strategic noise doesn't exist in the current data because teams haven't optimized against this specific model, but it would erode the feature's signal if the model were deployed publicly for betting markets.
**Fairness analysis - where the model predicts well and poorly.** Applying fairness concepts to driver/team tiers: overall accuracy is 59.2%, but this is not uniform. The model's accuracy for races won by the top-3 constructors is ~68–72%; for wins outside those three constructors it falls to ~35–40%. Equal Opportunity analysis: the true positive rate for identifying wins is higher for consistent frontrunners than for midfield drivers who win due to race-day circumstances - the model almost never predicts backmarker wins (near-zero false positive rate for that group), but this means it also systematically misses the upsets. Statistical Parity deliberately does not hold - the model assigns highly unequal predicted win probabilities - which is correct because the historical base rates are genuinely unequal, but it limits the model's usefulness for identifying dark-horse outcomes. Figure 22 shows the well/poorly structure directly: scatter is tight near P1–P5 (reliable where car quality is distinguishable) and wide in P7–P15 (genuine pre-race indeterminacy). Figure 26 (confusion matrix) shows the same pattern at the standings level - tight diagonal for top ranks, dispersed mid-table rows. Concrete examples: 2023 Verstappen races were correctly called with near-certainty; the 2022 Monaco GP and 2024 Singapore GP (both decided by strategy calls invisible from pre-race data) are the canonical mispredictions sitting far off the diagonal in Figure 22.
### Challenge Goal 2 - API Data Pipeline
**Goal.** Collect official FIA telemetry at scale without the rate-limited API creating a data acquisition bottleneck, and ensure all derived ML features are provably free of data leakage.
**Implementation.** The FastF1 API imposes a 200-request/hour rate limit. Covering five session types (FP1, FP2, FP3, Qualifying, Race) across 149 race rounds and eight seasons required 600+ requests total. The caching pipeline (cache_fastf1_data.py) manages this via a multi-threaded worker pool with exponential backoff and a JSON state file for resumable progress - long acquisition runs can be stopped and restarted without re-fetching already-completed sessions. All telemetry is stored in a local SQLite database. Six corrupted sessions from 2018–2019, missing qualifying times from wet-weather sessions, and the abbreviated 2020 calendar are all handled by rolling-average imputation with binary flags.
**Feature engineering and leakage prevention.** The feature engineering pipeline (feature_engineering.py) generates 54 ML-ready features with temporal ordering enforced through compute_rolling_features(race_index), which applies a boolean mask so no rolling statistic ever uses data from a race that hadn't yet happened at prediction time. For the 2018 LOSO fold - the first year in the cache, with no prior-season history available - rolling windows use only within-2018 data, producing shorter effective windows and explaining the lower 47.6% accuracy that year. The preprocessing pipeline (preprocess_data.py) is fully reproducible from the cached database, separating slow rate-limited acquisition from fast deterministic transformation. The full 54-feature list and all engineering decisions are documented in Appendix D.
